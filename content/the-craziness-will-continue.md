Since I work at an AI SaaS company (pejorative haha). I've come to read a lot of articles on AI in industry. Here are a collection of thoughts in no particular order that I've duct-taped together:

- LLMs are only currently working for people because they are _new_. You have gained hard won ground on your mental models. You are in an extraction phase and sacrificing everything at the altar of speed.
- Agentic programming is addictive since when it works you get dopamine. when it doesn't you get adrenaline (variable ratio reinforcement).
- Writing a code is mentally taxing while prompting isn't. You can keep going long past the point from which your judgement is degraded and end up pushing subpar work that gets abandoned.
- With programming you're planning along the way while with agents you have a plan upfront and it's difficult to change direction. You get speed while losing direction. Consider when the last time that you have known all the requirements upfront.
- Using agents turns you into a spectator. It's passive enough to feel like rest, active enough to keep you engaged. So you are never in a finished state. This also means that when you need to be a player you _can't_. You've only ever watched the sport, never played it.
- If you're new to a company and we cared about today's productivity we wouldn't have hired you at all. We would have just given it to a senior. So on some level every company cares about your growth. Or they just have enough tasks of your level to complete.
- You are either an A, B, or C player. We can only care about A's and B's. They get the reward. The rest get pushed out.
- Agents cause increasing complexity of systems, atrophying skills and vendor lock-in.
- It's not just another abstraction; people cannot think properly after using these tools. That type of thing didn't happen when perl was invented.
- Agents working well hinges on a skilled developer thinking critically and being comfortable at operating at the architectural level while being able to spot issues in thousands of LoC before they become a problem. AI is degrading this capability.
- You don't have the longevity of the 30+ years of friction that lead to deep understanding; they are relying on firm mental models.
- Paradox of supervision: effectively using agents requires supervision and supervising agents requires the very coding skills that atrophy from AI overuse.
- Speed is a natural byproduct of high aptitude. When it's forced it always leads to lower accuracy.
- What you say is often not what you mean. Agents fill in ambiguity with assumptions (or hallucinations) which leads to more review, more agent revisions and more disconnection from what is being created. The gap in your understanding of the codebase and your agent creates a negative spiral.
- I've never seen a dark factory post about their successes and how it panned out for them in the long term. Perhaps there is something to be said about that.
- With agents you're getting more gains for the company with negative gains for yourself. Companies are made of people and eventually this will cause a fail state.
- You have a responsibility to a system beyond just shipping. You want understanding of a system so that you can properly manage, extend, maintain. You should be aiming for understanding.
- Understanding does not work on a fixed clock. The day that you spend debugging something may have no material impact to the business. But you gained a deep understanding of some system along the way and are familiar with the code. You could have fixed it in an hour with an LLM and gained none of that. Businesses are choosing the LLM option by default every time.
- Cognitive surrender vs delegation: Every task that you handed to AI is by definition something that you either didn't want to do or couldn't do. The latter is quite scary!
- The complete brain twister of this all is that using the tool makes you worse at using the tool. I feel that this is a lot of the underlying tension behind the problem.
- The gap between skilled developers and unskilled developers is going to get wider depending on responsible use of AI.
- Almost every article I've read falls into the category of complaining about non-functional requirements being traded for functional requirements. Stuff like code quality, developer skill atrophy via overuse, maintainability etc being traded for functional requirements like speed and getting the thing now. Honestly I'm not surprised at all at the current state of things. Have you ever met a company that has talked about their values (NFR) past your onboarding? I've realized that overall companies are very unlikely to care unless you can convince them that some NFRs actually aid or overlap with their FRs. A lot of this can be seen by taking a longer time horizon which is difficult to do when your company sells stock instead of a product. So join or create a product company.
